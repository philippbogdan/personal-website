title: On interface density
description: A short argument for showing people more, not less.

Every interface I have loved was denser than its competitors. Not busier — denser.
The distinction matters, and most product teams collapse it.

Busy is when an interface asks you to look in several places at once and gives you
no reason to prefer one over another. Dense is when a great deal of information sits
in a small space and the eye still knows exactly where to land. Density without
hierarchy is noise. Hierarchy without density is a slideshow.

## The cost of the empty screen

The last decade of design orthodoxy has been a slow retreat from density. Whitespace
became a proxy for quality, and "clean" became a compliment you could give without
having looked closely at anything. The result is a class of software that feels
calm on a marketing page and exhausting in daily use, because every real task
now spans four screens that each hold one decision.

> The person using your tool for the ninetieth time is not the person you designed
> the onboarding for.

Expertise is the case that gets ignored. First use is legible, measurable, and easy
to demo. The ninetieth use is where the value actually accrues, and it is almost
never in the deck.

## What density buys

Three things, mostly:

- **Fewer round trips.** Information that shares a screen can be compared. Information
  split across screens has to be memorised.
- **A shape you can learn.** A dense layout stays still. You develop muscle memory for
  where things live, and that memory is worth more than any amount of guided discovery.
- **Honesty about the work.** Some tasks are genuinely complicated. Hiding that behind
  progressive disclosure does not simplify the task, it simplifies the screenshot.

The tools that people describe as fast are rarely the ones with the lowest latency.
They are the ones that let you do the next thing without moving.

## Where it goes wrong

Density fails when it is achieved by shrinking rather than by ordering. Setting
everything at twelve pixels is not density, it is compression, and it hurts.
Real density comes from a strict grid, a small number of type sizes used
consistently, and ruthless demotion of anything secondary to a genuinely
secondary weight.

The test I use: cover the screen, then look at it for one second and cover it again.
If you can say what the screen is *for*, it is dense. If you can only say what is
*on* it, it is busy.
